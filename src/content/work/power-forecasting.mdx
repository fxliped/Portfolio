## Context

Data center power utilization is a forecasting problem with money attached in both directions.
Over-provision and you strand capital; under-provision and you constrain the business. The
existing forecast carried **6.3% mean absolute error**, wide enough that planners padded
their decisions to absorb it.

The data underneath was fine: 500M+ hardware telemetry events a day. Getting it into a shape
anyone could plan against took six hours of manual assembly every week.

## The problem

Three gaps, and the modeling one wasn't the most valuable to close first.

- **The forecast was too coarse** to plan a single availability zone against.
- **The data path was manual**, so the forecast was only as fresh as someone's Wednesday.
- **Deviations were caught by eye.** S3 Glacier demand anomalies were monitored in Excel,
  which means they were found when someone happened to look.

## What I built

**ELT pipelines in dbt and Redshift.** 500M+ daily events modeled into BI-ready structures.
dbt mattered less for the transformations than for making them *reviewable*, so the definitions
stopped living in one analyst's saved queries. Weekly reporting went from six hours to thirty
minutes.

**An ensemble forecasting model.** Utilization has several regimes at once: steady baseline
draw, seasonal shape, and step changes from deployments. Single models compromised across all
three; an ensemble let components carry what each was good at. That's where most of the
**6.3% → 1.9%** improvement came from.

Applied per availability zone, the tighter forecast surfaced sustained under-usage that had
been invisible inside the wider error band, informing a **$400K initiative across 12 AZs**.

**Statistical anomaly detection** on global S3 Glacier demand, replacing the Excel workflow
and catching **14 anomalies a month** before they affected capacity.

## The hard part

**Beating the baseline honestly.** It's easy to post a better error number by evaluating on a
window that flatters the model. A defensible 1.9% meant being strict about holdout periods
that *included* the step changes, the regimes where 6.3% was actually costing decisions.

**False-positive rate decides whether anomaly detection survives.** A detector that pages
someone weekly gets ignored within a month, and then it's worse than the spreadsheet it
replaced. Tuning for actionable alerts rather than sensitivity was most of that work.

## What I'd do differently

Ship the pipeline before the model. The forecast got the attention because it was the
interesting problem, but six-hours-to-thirty-minutes is what planners felt week to week,
and it was the prerequisite for the model being usable at all.
